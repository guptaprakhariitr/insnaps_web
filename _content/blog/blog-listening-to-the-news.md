# Can You Just Listen to the News Instead of Reading It?

_Published 1 August 2026 · Updated 1 August 2026_

**The short answer:** Yes, and a lot of people already do. The Reuters Institute's *Digital News Report 2025* puts podcasts at around **10% of news access**, with **73% of podcast users saying podcasts help them understand topics more deeply**, while news consumption via social video climbed from **52% in 2020 to 65% in 2025** and **44% of 18–24s** now name social media as their main source of news. Listening works well for context and badly for scanning. InSnaps' **Pulse** mode sits in between: auto-playing cards with an image, a typed caption and **device text-to-speech** narration, so you can put the phone down and still get through a session. Be clear about what that is: it's your phone's built-in synthetic voice reading text, not human voice actors or recorded audio journalism.

## Listening is a different activity, not a lazier one

People frame audio news as reading-for-people-who-can't-be-bothered. That's wrong in an interesting way. Listening and reading fail at opposite things.

Reading is **random-access**. Your eye skips, skims, backtracks, jumps to the number in the third paragraph, and abandons the piece in two seconds if the first line is filler. That's enormously efficient for triage.

Listening is **serial**. You can't skim a voice. You get it in the order it was written, at a fixed pace, and skipping is clumsy. Terrible for triage; genuinely good at the thing reading is worst at: staying with a story long enough to absorb the context. Which is roughly what the *Digital News Report 2025* finding reflects: **73% of podcast users say podcasts help them understand topics more deeply**. Not "faster." Deeper.

So the practical rule is not to replace reading with listening. Use audio for the parts of your day where reading is impossible (commuting, cooking, walking the dog, the gym) and accept that you'll cover fewer stories more thoroughly. That's the trade, and it's a good one if you take it deliberately.

## What the numbers actually say about how people consume news now

Worth grounding this, because the argument is usually made without numbers.

From the Reuters Institute's *Digital News Report 2025*: podcasts account for roughly **10% of news access**. Real, but nowhere near dominant, so anyone announcing that audio has replaced text is overselling. Meanwhile news consumption via **social video rose from 52% in 2020 to 65% in 2025**, and **44% of 18–24-year-olds** name social media as their main source of news.

Read together, the picture isn't "audio is winning." It's that the **card-and-clip format** is winning: short, visual, autoplaying, narrated. Pure text is losing ground fastest among the youngest readers. Audio-only podcasts are a strong but niche slice; audio *attached to a visual card* is the format that captured the last five years. That's the gap products like Pulse aim at: the social-video format applied to news, without the rage-bait attached.

## How Pulse works, described honestly

InSnaps' Pulse mode plays your feed as a sequence of auto-advancing cards. Each card has an image, a caption typed onto the screen as it plays, and narration produced by your **device's text-to-speech engine**: the same system-level speech synthesiser that reads your phone's accessibility content. You can watch it, or lock the phone and just listen.

What that means in practice:

- **It's synthetic, and it sounds synthetic.** Modern on-device voices are far better than they were, but this is not a presenter, not a reporter reading their own piece, and not produced audio with tape or ambience. If you love the craft of a well-made news podcast, Pulse is not that and won't feel like it.

## Who should actually use audio news, and who shouldn't

**Use it if** your reading time is genuinely constrained by your hands and eyes being busy; if you retain spoken information well; if you'd otherwise read nothing on a commute; or if reading on a screen is difficult for you for accessibility reasons. Synthetic narration was built for exactly that and it's the least-discussed reason it's valuable.

**Skip it if** you triage heavily. If your reading style is scanning twenty headlines to pick two, audio will drive you up a wall, because the whole point of scanning is skipping and audio makes skipping expensive. Also skip if you specifically want the *craft*: reported audio features, interview tape, an editor's structure. A synthetic voice reading a summary is a genuinely different product and pretending otherwise is how apps lose trust.

## What it won't do

Pulse's narration is **device text-to-speech**. It is not a human voice actor, not a presenter, and not recorded audio journalism, and it will not sound like one. It won't do interviews, tape, ambience or a host's judgement about what matters. It won't make a long investigative piece into a listenable feature; that's editing work, and nothing automatic does it.

Beyond audio, the honest limitations on InSnaps generally: it's an **early-stage indie app**. The interface is available in **11 languages, but all news content is English-only**. There's no translation layer, and narration therefore reads English text regardless of your interface language. The **free tier opens 13 of the 32 topic domains**. All source counts and coverage claims are **first-party**, taken from InSnaps' own store listings and site rather than an independent audit. It does not do bias comparison across sources; Ground News is better at that and it isn't a feature InSnaps replicates. And no format, audio included, fixes the underlying supply problem where local reporting has disappeared.
