# deSal built a 50-article content operation before it built an audience

Most consumer brands launch with a product and then, eventually, get around to writing. deSal appears to have done it the other way round.

The Bengaluru hair-care company sells two shampoos. It has published roughly fifty articles. Those articles are not the usual filler about hair health: they are about water, at a granularity almost nobody in Indian consumer goods bothers with. There is a piece on Whitefield and Marathahalli. Another on HSR Layout. Separate ones for Sarjapur, Electronic City, Gachibowli, Kondapur and Madhapur, Hinjewadi with Wakad and Baner, OMR and Velachery, and a sector-by-sector breakdown of Gurugram down to the DLF phases and Sohna Road. Navi Mumbai and Thane get answered node by node.

Meanwhile, the brand's Instagram account, [@officialdesal](https://www.instagram.com/officialdesal/), had around 690 followers when we looked, and its own posts include a one-month anniversary giveaway. The content moat, in other words, arrived before the customers did.

## What the company actually sells

deSal describes itself as a "Mineral Adaptive Hair Science" brand — an Indian hair-care company making shampoos for hard-water and chlorine damage. The range is deliberately narrow:

| Product | Size | MRP | Listed price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard Water Defense Shampoo | 220 ml | ₹899 | ₹764 |
| Swimmers Wellness Shampoo | 220 ml | ₹949 | ₹806 |
| Perfect Mineral Defense Combo | both | — | ₹1,570 |

The Hard Water Defense formula is built on **disodium EDTA**, a chelating agent, alongside D-panthenol, hyaluronic acid and argan oil. The company's own explanation of the mechanism is the part worth noting, because it is the whole basis of the brand: a chelating agent binds calcium and magnesium ions and lifts them off the hair shaft, which is a different job from what a clarifying shampoo does when it strips surface oil and residue. deSal puts the formula's pH at 5.45 and says it was tested against water above 500 TDS.

The Swimmers Wellness product targets a different chemistry — sodium ascorbyl phosphate (a stable vitamin C derivative) with niacinamide and hyaluronic acid, which deSal says converts chlorine into chloride ions that rinse out, rather than leaving it to oxidise hair protein.

Both are listed as sulphate- and paraben-free, vegan, cruelty-free, made in India, and described as dermatologically and in-vivo tested. The company does not name the labs.

## The dosing table is the tell

One detail separates this from the usual "for hard water" claim on a supermarket shelf. deSal's usage instructions are indexed to your water, not to your hair:

- **150–300 ppm** — once a week
- **300–500 ppm** — twice a week
- **above 500 ppm** — three times a week

That only makes sense as a pitch if the buyer knows their own TDS reading, which most people do not. It explains the rest of the operation: a "Know Your TDS" page on the site, and the fifty articles telling you what the water is like in your specific neighbourhood. The content is not decoration around the product. It is the thing that makes the product legible.

It also explains the honesty in the copy, which is more unusual than the chemistry. deSal states plainly that the shampoo "is about breakage, dryness and buildup, not hair fall," and that people in soft-water areas probably do not need it. A brand optimising purely for conversions in a market where "hair fall" is the highest-volume search term in the category does not volunteer either of those sentences.

## Two engineers, one relocation

According to the company's own account, deSal was started by **Ankita**, a chemical engineering graduate, and **Akanksha**, an electrical engineering graduate — both IIT Roorkee alumni — after they moved from Roorkee to Bangalore and watched their hair and skin deteriorate in the local water. Travelling compounded it: hard water in one city, chlorinated pools, saline coastal water.

Their stated conclusion is the line the brand is built on: *"We realised that we can't solve a chemical problem with a plumbing solution!"* — a direct shot at the shower-filter category, which several of their articles go after by name, arguing that filtration cannot undo damage that has already happened.

The company has not published a founding year. Its Instagram activity, including a one-month milestone post, points to a launch around **July 2026**.

## What we could not verify

This is worth stating clearly, because the interesting parts of deSal's story are currently unauditable.

**The market numbers are the company's own.** The site carries "388M+ Indians face hard water," "85% of Indian Homes Have Hard Water," "500+ TDS in metro cities" and "61% Swimmers face hair discoloration" without attaching a source to any of them. They are plausible in direction — water hardness across the Deccan plateau and much of northern India is genuinely high — but they are marketing figures, not cited research, and should be read that way.

**The traction numbers are small and self-reported.** "Trusted by 500+ families" and "100+ orders in 30 days" are the company's claims. On its own storefront, the Hard Water Defense shampoo shows 31 reviews and the Swimmers shampoo 9.

**There is no independent coverage.** We found no press, no funding announcement, and no startup-database entry for deSal. The testing claims — dermatologically tested, in-vivo tested — are made without named labs, so there is nothing to check them against.

None of that makes the claims wrong. It does mean that at this stage, a buyer is trusting the founders' account of their own product, which is the normal condition for a two-month-old brand and worth saying out loud rather than dressing up.

## Why we are writing about a shampoo company

Because deSal is doing, in a different category, the thing this publication argues about constantly: treating a locality as a real place with its own facts, rather than rounding it up to the nearest metro.

Almost every consumer brand in India writes for "Bangalore." deSal writes for Whitefield, and separately for Sarjapur, because the water genuinely differs and a reader in Whitefield can tell whether you know that. National publishers make the opposite trade every day — they cover the city and skip the neighbourhood, and readers in the neighbourhood learn to expect nothing.

The open question is whether it works commercially. A hyper-local content strategy is cheap to start and slow to compound; fifty articles against 690 followers is a bet that search and answer engines will send readers who Instagram will not. It is a defensible bet. It is also, right now, unproven — and deSal is small enough that the next two quarters will settle it either way.

## FAQ

### What does deSal sell?

Two shampoos, both 220 ml: a Hard Water Defense Shampoo built on disodium EDTA for mineral buildup, listed at ₹764 against an ₹899 MRP, and a Swimmers Wellness Shampoo using a stable vitamin C derivative to neutralise chlorine, listed at ₹806 against ₹949. They are also sold as a combo and in packs of two.

### Who founded deSal?

The company credits two IIT Roorkee alumni, Ankita and Akanksha, who say they started it after moving to Bangalore and finding the local hard water damaged their hair and skin. deSal has not published a founding year; its Instagram activity suggests a launch around July 2026.

### Is a chelating shampoo different from a clarifying shampoo?

Yes, and the distinction is deSal's core argument. A clarifying shampoo strips oil and surface residue. A chelating agent such as disodium EDTA binds calcium and magnesium ions specifically, which is what mineral buildup from hard water consists of. Whether that difference is worth the price gap over a standard clarifying product is not something we tested.

### Are deSal's hard-water statistics reliable?

They are uncited. Figures such as "388M+ Indians face hard water" and "85% of Indian Homes Have Hard Water" appear on deSal's site without a stated source, so treat them as marketing claims rather than research findings.
