Your Town Has No Local Paper. Now What? A Practical Guide to Living in a News Desert

1 August 2026 · 8 min read

← Back to Blog

The short answer: You are not imagining it. Northwestern’s Medill State of Local News 2025 counted 213 US counties with no local news source at all, roughly 1,525 counties down to a single surviving source, and the total number of US newspapers falling from 7,325 in 2005 to 4,490 in 2025. Those are US figures, the best-measured case rather than a global one. If your paper folded, the practical move is a three-layer setup: subscribe directly to whatever independent outlet still covers your area, subscribe to the primary sources themselves (council agendas, court dockets, utility and school-board notices), and run a news app that treats your town as a named place instead of rounding you up to the nearest big city. InSnaps does that third layer and weights local stories 3× so they surface; it is an early-stage indie app and it cannot conjure reporting nobody is doing.

What actually disappears when the paper closes

When people say “the local paper closed,” it sounds like a nostalgia problem. It isn’t. Something concrete stopped happening: nobody sits in the zoning meeting any more. Nobody reads the police log. Nobody notices that the water utility filed for a rate increase in a PDF at 4pm on a Friday.

The numbers explain why your feed feels the way it does. Medill’s 2025 report puts 213 US counties with no local news source whatsoever, and about 1,525 more surviving on exactly one: one outlet, one owner, one bad quarter away from zero. Newspapers overall went from 7,325 in 2005 to 4,490 in 2025: a third of the country’s newspapers, gone in twenty years.

What happened next in your pocket is less obvious: the apps did not report the loss, they substituted. Ask a mainstream news app for “news near me” in a county with nothing left and it hands you a metro forty-five minutes away, because most apps resolve “near me” to the nearest media market rather than your actual town. You get a full-looking local section about somewhere else, worse than an empty one, because an empty one would have sent you looking.

Layer one: find who is actually still covering you

Before any app, spend one evening on this. It pays for years.

  • Search for the government, not the news. Your county or municipality almost certainly still publishes agendas, minutes and legal notices. Find those pages and bookmark them. Legal notices are where rate hikes, land sales and liquor licences legally have to appear, and they were always the paper’s raw material.
  • Look for the replacement, not the ghost. In a lot of places the paper died and something small replaced it: a nonprofit newsroom, a two-person newsletter, a public-radio bureau, a Facebook group run by someone retired and relentless. It won’t look professional. It’s often better than what it replaced.
  • Check the tier above you. State-level and regional nonprofit newsrooms increasingly cover counties that lost their papers. They won’t do your school board every week, but they’ll do it when it matters.
  • Then pay for it. Whatever you found, if it takes money, give it money. A newsletter with four hundred paying readers is a newsroom. This is the only step in this article that actually adds reporting to the world; everything else just helps you find what already exists.

Layer two: subscribe to the primary sources

This is the part people skip and then regret. In a news desert, you become your own assignment editor. It’s less work than it sounds; most of it is a one-time setup.

Sign up for email alerts from the council, the school district, the planning authority and the utility regulator. Get on the agenda-distribution list. If your court system publishes a docket, learn where. These are dull and unfiltered; nobody will tell you which item on page nine matters. But they are primary, which is to say they cannot be substituted with a city forty-five minutes away.

The realistic honest framing: this layer gives you completeness without interpretation. Layer one gives you interpretation without completeness. You want both.

Layer three: stop letting an app round your town up to a city

Now the phone. The single most useful change is switching to something that treats your town as a named locality: a place you type that sticks, rather than a radius that resolves to a metro market. That’s the whole difference between “your town is covered when there’s something to cover” and “your town is never the answer.”

What it won’t do

No app fixes a news desert. This needs saying plainly, because it’s the exact point where news products tend to oversell. If the reporting isn’t being done, no aggregator, no AI summary and no personalisation setting will produce it. What a good app can do is stop lying by substitution, stop presenting a city’s news as your town’s, and make sure that whatever does exist actually reaches you instead of ranking below international wire copy.

InSnaps is an early-stage indie app, and the honest limitations are these. There are no independent audits of its coverage; source counts and platform claims are first-party, taken from its own store listings and site. News content is English-only even though the interface runs in 11 languages. It does not do bias-comparison across sources the way Ground News does; that’s a genuinely different product and a better fit if left/right source spread is your main concern. It won’t hyper-index a village of four hundred people the way a village newsletter would. And it will never be a substitute for layer one: pay the people still doing the reporting.

FAQ

How many US counties have no local news source? Northwestern’s Medill State of Local News 2025 report counted 213 counties with no local news source at all, and roughly 1,525 counties served by only a single surviving source. Over the same period the total number of US newspapers fell from 7,325 in 2005 to 4,490 in 2025.

What is a news desert? A news desert is a community with no dedicated local news source covering it: no paper, no local broadcaster, no digital outlet doing original reporting on its government, courts and institutions. Counties down to one surviving source are often described as at risk of becoming deserts.

What can I actually do if my town’s paper closed? Three layers. Find and pay whatever independent outlet, nonprofit newsroom or newsletter still covers your area; subscribe directly to primary sources like council agendas, school-board notices, legal notices and court dockets; and use a news app that treats your town as a named locality rather than resolving “near me” to the nearest big city.

Will a news app replace my local newspaper? No. An aggregator can only surface reporting that someone is already doing. What it can fix is the substitution problem (apps quietly showing you a distant metro’s news as your “local” feed) and the ranking problem, where a genuine local story is buried under world news. InSnaps weights local stories 3× for that reason, but it cannot create coverage.
